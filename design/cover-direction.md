# Self-Driving Jazz — cover art direction

Every project gets a square record-sleeve cover. The covers form one catalogue
from an institution that should not exist: a British–Swiss production-music
library, active roughly 1958–1975. Think KPM, Bruton and De Wolfe library sleeves,
Swiss International Style posters, 1960s–70s Sainsbury's own-brand packaging and
Penguin/Pelican covers — calm, intelligent, slightly magical modernism with one
dry joke hidden in plain sight.

## House rules (every cover)

- **Era and craft:** it must look designed and printed between 1958 and 1975 —
  offset or screen print on paper, real halftone, slight misregistration, paper
  grain, gentle wear. Nothing should look digital, photorealistic-modern, or
  post-1980.
- **Image first:** a single visual concept drawn from what the project actually
  is, committed to completely and presented deadpan. The image carries the
  cover and fills most of it; it should reward a second look.
- **Quiet typography:** the project title, spelled exactly, small and precise in
  a period grotesk (Helvetica, Univers, Akzidenz) — a caption-sized line or a
  modest block, never a headline that dominates the sleeve. A tiny catalogue
  mark is allowed; no other text.
- **Restraint:** limited flat colour (two or three inks), generous space, a clear
  grid. Square 1:1.

## Series

Each candidate is assigned one series. Work inside its idiom with a fresh
composition — the series is a vocabulary, not a layout to copy. Choose the
palette for the idea: avoid defaulting to navy grounds, and never fall back on a
grid of repeated photographs.

### Single photograph
One grainy black-and-white photograph of one object, gesture or place, cropped
with intent and given room on the paper; one flat ink block or rule beside it.

### Own-brand packaging
1960s–70s supermarket own-label design: big flat fields of one bold colour and a
simple stylised illustration or abstract shape of the subject that dominates the
pack; the title small and plain, as if naming a product.

### Swiss poster
Strict grid and pure geometric abstraction — shapes, rules and colour fields do
the work; the title is a small, exact element placed on the grid.

### Period future
One optimistic 1960s vision of the future, drawn as a single diagrammatic
object or scene in flat cut shapes and halftone, on a light, warm or vivid
ground.

### Pelican cover
Small photographic or engraved cut-out collaged into flat colour areas with a
witty juxtaposition; a small, quietly placed title.

### Op-art pattern
A single systematic pattern (lines, dots, modules) that bends, breaks or repeats
to embody the idea; title small and precise.

### Scientific plate
An engraved or lithographed specimen illustration — botanical, anatomical,
mechanical — of the idea as if it were a natural object, on plain paper.

### Cut paper
Torn and cut coloured paper shapes, Matisse-flat or children's-book bold,
arranged into one clear image with visible paper edges.

### Technical drawing
Line-only engineering or architectural drawing — section, exploded view,
isometric — in one ink, with one area filled in a second colour.

### Typographic sculpture
The image made from letterforms or punctuation themselves (not the title):
large type set as shape, texture or pattern in two inks.

### Ink and brush
Loose single-colour brush drawing or sumi-like ink wash, one confident gesture
carrying the idea, lots of untouched paper.

## Never

- Anything that reads as 1980s–2000s or contemporary: neon, airbrush, chrome,
  glossy 3D, photorealistic modern product shots, pixel or digital fonts,
  stickers, shrink-wrap, cyberpunk, glitch, glowing AI imagery.
- Affordance text: captions, labels, callouts, UI chrome, HUD, warnings, status
  lines, fake metadata, slogans.
- Copying an existing album cover, artist, brand, or the reference covers.
